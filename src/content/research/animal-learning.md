We build quantitative theories of how animals learn and of how their brains implement this learning. We work closely with experimental groups, among them Sam Sober's lab at Emory, which studies birdsong, and Will Ryu's lab, which studies the worm *C. elegans*.

In songbirds, we found that [precisely timed spike patterns](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1611734114) in vocal muscles control behavior at millisecond resolution, so motor commands are carried by spike timing as well as by firing rate. Our recent work shows that this [millisecond-scale motor coding precedes sensorimotor learning](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.09.27.615500). A [non-Gaussian Bayesian theory of sensorimotor learning](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1713020115) explains why birds discount rare large vocal errors and quantitatively models the distribution of song statistics. With the Sober lab, we now study how motor learning generalizes in birdsong.

In *C. elegans*, [quantitative modeling of operant conditioning](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2215191120) revealed separate food-associated and food-independent learning pathways, which experiments then confirmed. We also ask what neural recordings can and cannot reveal about circuits. For example, [randomly connected networks](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1007875) reproduce the selectivity and population decoding properties usually attributed to structured wiring, so the reported experiments cannot distinguish the two.

We also study [intrinsic motivation](https://doi.org/10.1103/PRXLife.2.033009), behavioral drives that emerge from an agent's interaction with its surroundings, without any externally supplied reward. Agents that maximize their control over their own future, a quantity called empowerment, generate purposeful behavior on their own. When several such agents interact, a [multi-agent extension of empowerment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.21155) gives rise to characteristic modes of group organization, for example in a pair of agents coupled by a tendon and in a controllable flock. Intrinsic motivation can thus drive collaborative behavior of groups, as well as the behavior of individual agents.
