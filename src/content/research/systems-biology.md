Living systems are built from enormous numbers of interacting molecules and cells, and yet many of their functions can be captured by simple models. We look for such coarse-grained, functionally accurate descriptions, from signaling inside single cells to the regulation of whole-body physiology, and we increasingly use them to connect systems biology to clinical care.

Cells sense and respond to their environment with noisy molecular machinery. With Andre Levchenko's lab, we [measured how much information a mammalian signaling pathway transmits](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1204553), and found that a single pathway carries barely more than one bit. We also showed that [communication through gap junctions](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1516503113) lets cell ensembles sense shallow chemical gradients far more precisely than isolated cells can. Our recent work shows that knowing the statistical structure of concentration profiles, such as the predictable spatiotemporal patterns seen during development, lets cells use current binding events to [predict future concentrations](https://doi.org/10.1103/b6b7-s4hx), and so sense them more precisely than the classical Berg–Purcell limit allows. Earlier, we developed [ARACNE](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2105-7-S1-S7), a widely used method for reconstructing gene regulatory networks, and a theory of [optimal signal processing in small, noisy biochemical networks](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0001077).

In physiology, we focus on the regulation of blood glucose by insulin, the failure of which produces diabetes. Why do animals use hormones at all? We showed that [existing mathematical models of glucose regulation](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.08.24.746760) can be controlled equally well without any hormones, so they cannot explain why hormones are needed. A minimal model of glucose variations between organs shows that control based on local glucose measurements faces severe trade-offs, which hormonal signals from the pancreas overcome. With Priyathama Vellanki's group in Emory's Division of Endocrinology, we build minimal dynamical models of clinical data. These revealed that [remission in ketosis-prone diabetes](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.100193) requires more than one mechanism of reversible β-cell inactivation, and the collaboration now continues in a [clinical study of diabetes remission](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06699810).
